Case Study · Federal Web Application

Modernizing FEMA's Mitigation Planning Portal

MPP 2.0 consolidates two outdated systems into a user-centered platform, giving federal planners a clearer, faster way to manage hazard mitigation plans across all ten FEMA regions.

Role
Solo UX Designer
Client
FEMA (Federal)
Timeline
4 months active
Platform
Web Application

Background

The Mitigation Planning Portal (MPP) is a web application developed by FEMA to track and report hazard mitigation plans and related data elements across all 10 regions. Users can create mitigation plans, manage compliance, and oversee funding across a national network of states and local jurisdictions.

"Consolidated space of record for mitigation plan status."

— Stakeholder workshop, FEMA

Understanding the Users

MPP comprises a wide range of FEMA users, each with varying levels of administrative access. Users are organized into three primary groups: Federal, Regional, and State, each with a unique set of access.

To cover the complexity and design process in greater depth, this case study focuses on federal users.

Primary Focus
Federal
Full edit access across all 10 regions. The most demanding user — highest data scope, highest stakes for accuracy.
Regional
Data quality oversight across all regions. Responsible for enforcing entry standards.
State
Scoped to assigned region or state. Mirror the Federal permission tiers within their geographic boundary.

The design work centered on the Federal Read & Write user — the role with the broadest scope and the most friction in the existing system. Meet Jamie:

"I shouldn't have to open Excel to know where a plan stands."

Pain Points

I learned from the stakeholder workshops that several user pain points were overlooked during the transition from the Legacy version to MPP 1.0, and the updates were completed without resolving many of the problems in the legacy version.

01 Broken user flow

The status dashboard provided a high-level view of plan status, but did not offer a direct path to the plans they were working on. Users had to navigate via search to resume their work.

02 Confusing review process

The current design lacks a reliable way to log their activity or leave comments tied to the plan they are working on.

03 Can't easily track updates

Users can either receive all notifications for a plan or none at all. For those working with 100 or more, it becomes overwhelming, making it nearly impossible to prioritize.

"Manually looking up and entering jurisdiction data can be tedious."

— Stakeholder workshop, FEMA

Legacy designs screenshots

Status Dashboard (Landing Page) - The landing page gave analysts a regional overview of plan statuses at a glance. It was a useful starting point but not a complete workflow. The design assumed users would navigate to individual plans through search, with no shortcut back to work already in progress.
Plan Detail - The plan detail page tried to show everything at once: basic plan information, funding status, and jurisdiction data all competing for space in a dense view. This made it difficult for analysts to focus on what mattered most at any point in their workflow.

Ideation

Design directions emerged directly from the pain points covered in stakeholder workshops. Working through rounds of client reviews, each feature proposal was grounded in a specific gap in the existing workflow.

Pain point 01

Analysts had no way to save their plans. They had to manually repeat search process to resume work which was a major pain point that came across with a 100 or more plans.

Flag Plans

A way to mark any plan and return to it directly from the dashboard.

Pain point 02

The all-or-nothing notification model overwhelmed analysts managing large number of plans. Analysts receiving every update for every plan made prioritization nearly impossible.

Alert Preferences

Granular, per-plan notification customization feature that let analysts follow only the updates they need.

Pain point 03

Plan detail pages tried to show everything at once which includes, funding, status, and jurisdiction data all competing for limited space in a single dense view.

Jurisdiction Status tab

A dedicated tab that gives jurisdiction data room to breathe, reducing cognitive load for analysts reviewing large datasets.

Final Designs

The modernization effort required to keep a lot of the existing structure. I treated this constraint as an opportunity, ensuring every change addressed a specific user need and could be justified to stakeholders.

Not all proposals were included in the final version. This case study documents the design I advocated for, including decisions that were ultimately removed.

6A

Flag Plans

Introduced a feature that lets users mark any plan and return to it directly from the dashboard, without reconstructing a search for work already in progress.

Naming the feature

From Save, Bookmark, Heart Icon, Add to Favorites to Flag Plans

The label went through several months of iteration before landing on "Flag Plans." Early candidates, such as "Save," "Bookmark," "Add to Favorites," etc., were ruled out because they carried a social media vibe that felt misaligned with the platform's tone. The stakeholders wanted it to feel like a serious data management tool, not an everyday social app. "Flag" worked because it already existed in the federal platform, ‘flagging a case’, ‘flagging a record’, and implied immediate attention.

6B

Jurisdiction Status tab

Separated the Jurisdiction Status table into its own dedicated tab, giving the data room to breathe.

Framing the decision

How it was positioned to the client

Rather than taking this as a visual cleanup, the decision was shaped around reducing cognitive load. Sectioning the dense content helps users process information faster and reduces the likelihood of input errors.

6C

Alert Preferences

The client-approved alert version used a modal pop-up triggered by a bell icon within a dense data table. Each time a planner configured alerts for a plan, they lost their place in the list, disrupting context instead of supporting it.

In most government applications, modals are typically associated with errors or important messages. Using a modal suggests urgency when users are simply configuring preferences.

The redesign

The split-panel approach

The redesigned Alert Preferences page uses a split-panel layout, with a card list of saved plans on the left and an inline settings panel on the right. Selecting a plan opens its alert configuration without leaving or interrupting the list view.

Each plan card displays a summary of active alerts allowing planners to quickly identify which plans have alerts configured.

Outcomes

This project was delivered without formal usability testing or post-launch analytics which is a common reality in out team. The reflection below captures what I learned from the process itself: the facilitation, the trade-offs, and the decisions I'd approach differently.

What I'd Do Differently

Would do again
Anchor every design decision to a specific stakeholder quote or workshop finding
Use the persona as a decision-making tool, not just a deliverable
Document the facilitation work more thoroughly
Advocate for my rationale even when the stakeholder's decision goes another way
Would do differently
Establish a baseline usability benchmark earlier, even informally
Push harder to resolve stakeholder conflicts before they affect delivery
Get alignment on visual direction in week one, not month two
Involve a second designer for peer review on key decision points

This project reinforced a key lesson: in enterprise and government UX, the most challenging problems often stem from competing stakeholder priorities, unclear mandates, and slow decision-making. Knowing when to advocate, reframe, or accept a direction is as important as the design work itself.
