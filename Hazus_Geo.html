01
Context

Context

Hazus is a hazard analysis generating platform used by federal agencies, urban planners, emergency managers, and hazard mitigation professionals to assess risk from natural disasters.

Despite its importance, the existing workflow for conducting location-based risk analysis is built on an outdated system that has not kept pace with modern user experience standards.

02
Problem

Problem

Defining the geographic boundary is the first step when creating a hazard analysis in Hazus. Analysts must specify the exact region they want to study, which can range from an entire county to a small census block.

In the existing system, selecting these boundaries relied heavily on ArcGIS-based hierarchical navigation. Users were required to drill down through multiple geographic levels in a rigid sequence, often mirroring the underlying GIS data structure rather than the analyst's actual workflow.

This approach created unnecessary complexity. Analysts who already knew the geographic unit they needed still had to navigate through multiple intermediate layers, and users without GIS knowledge often struggled to understand how to reach the desired boundary.

Core problems identified
Unconventional design standards — The interface was built around complex GIS design patterns rather than modern web application standards. This created a steep learning curve for new users and made simple tasks unnecessarily difficult.
Rigid hierarchical navigation — Selecting a geographic boundary requires users to drill down through multiple administrative layers that mirror the underlying GIS data structure. Analysts who already knew where to select they needed still had to navigate through unnecessary intermediate steps.
Knowledge barrier for non-GIS users — Many intended users of Hazus, such as planners and emergency managers, do not have formal GIS training. However, the existing workflow assumed familiarity with GIS concepts, making it difficult for non-technical users to define their study area.
03
Research Approach

Research Approach

Traditional user interviews were not conducted for this project. Instead, the team worked through a structured process of client workshop sessions and collecting business requirements, translating domain expertise and organizational priorities directly into design decisions.

The team identified three primary user groups to design for across the feedback process:

Government staff who needed to access location data without extensive GIS knowledge.
Technical specialists, hazard analysts and urban planners, who needed speed and directness.
Emergency managers who require clarity and confidence in their location selections under pressure.
User needs

View hazard information within a map-based interface to better understand the geographic context of the analysis.

Select a geographic area directly on the map to initiate or capture hazard analysis.

Understand potential impacts within a jurisdiction to plan mitigation, response, and recovery strategies.

Design Opportunity

Opportunity

To simplify this process, I explored an interactive map that would allow analysts to first select a state and then directly choose the geographic boundary type they needed, such as county, community, or census block, without requiring a strict hierarchical drill-down.

05
Key Design Decision

Key Design
Decision

The most significant design challenge in this project was how users select their geographic location. If the user could not easily identify and select the correct jurisdiction, the analysis they get would be inaccurate or incomplete.

The current approach

The existing system used a layered, hierarchical model. Users had to navigate downward through a geographic pyramid: state → county → jurisdiction — even when they already knew the exact location they wanted to select.

This structure became even more inefficient when users needed to select multiple areas. For example, selecting several census tracts across neighboring communities required repeating the navigation process from the top of the hierarchy.

As a result, the interaction was slow and frustrating for experienced analysts and confusing for users without GIS knowledge.

The design exploration

Early wireframes explored a refined version of the pyramid model, cleaner, more modern, but structurally the same as the current design. Through continued stakeholder discussions and design refinements, it became clear that the core issue was not simply the design. It was all about how users think and process geographic information. The interaction mirrored the structure of complex datasets rather than the way analysts think about geographic boundaries.

The Insight
"When people think about where they live, they don't start from the top of a geographic pyramid. They already know the place they're looking for. I wanted the interactive map to reflect that mental model instead of forcing users through a rigid hierarchy."
The solution

Instead of forcing users to follow a strict hierarchical drill-down, the redesigned workflow allows analysts to first select a state and then choose the geographic boundary type they want to work with, such as county, community, or census block.

This approach removes unnecessary navigation steps and aligns the interaction with how analysts define their study areas.

06
Outcomes & Reflection

Outcomes &
Reflection

The redesign transformed OpenHazus from a tool that required specialist knowledge to engage with into an interface accessible to our user groups regardless of GIS background. The changes delivered through the design process addressed each of the original pain points identified at the project's workshop.

What was achieved
Modern design standards adopted throughout the map, the tool now aligns with modern web conventions, significantly reducing the onboarding barrier for new users.
Direct jurisdiction access replaced the legacy pyramid flow; users can reach their target location in a single step, eliminating unnecessary navigation for both expert and non-specialist users.

What I learned

This project made me think that working within constraints, whether research constraints like the absence of user research, or technical constraints in implementation, doesn't have to mean compromising the quality of the design. It means being more deliberate about the logic behind every design decision.

Collaborating with other UX designers also sharpened my ability to align direction quickly and present a unified point of view to stakeholders, even when internal perspectives differed.

If I could do one thing differently: 'I would have validated the jurisdiction model earlier in the process.
