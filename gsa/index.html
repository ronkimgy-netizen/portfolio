01 — Overview

Overview

SAM.gov is the U.S. federal government's central platform owned by the General Services Administration (GSA) for contract opportunities, grants, and entity registration. The GSA initiated a modernization effort to consolidate 10 independent federal services into a single, unified experience.

My Role

As a designer, I reviewed the current state of the search results page, examining its application across various domains to identify key areas of pain points and potential improvements. My primary objectives focused on enhancing the site’s visual appeal and usability.

02 — The Challenge

Challenge

There was no unified search.

Before the SAM.gov got merged, each of the 10 legacy systems had it's own search engines. When these systems merged, the existing search had poor usability for users due to outdated design patterns that ignored current industry standards and user expectations.

Users who needed to find a contract data or look up wage information had to use different services.

Usability Testing

Formal user research was not available during the client engagement. To validate design decisions for this case study, I conducted informal usability testing with two subject matter experts after the project was completed.

Following were the key findings from the user testing.

  • Search results were hard to scan — content felt grouped arbitrarily, making it difficult to extract relevant information quickly
  • Key filter options were invisible until activated — users didn't know what filtering was possible until they clicked into a dropdown
  • Long dropdown lists offered no way to preview or narrow options before committing

04 — Key Problems

Pain Points

01

Ambiguous result grouping

Search results appeared as a flat, visually undifferentiated list spanning multiple domains. Without clear grouping or visual hierarchy, results were difficult to scan, particularly for users seeking specific record types within extensive result sets.

02

Filter with too many unnecessary actions

The existing filter wasn’t simple enough to digest the complex dataset displayed in the search results. Users were forced to go through complicated steps without proper visual elements, hierarchy, and groupings, and they experienced pain points when using the filters.

05 — Ideation

Ideation

Wireframes

Three layout directions were explored before arriving at the final design. Each concept addressed part of the problem but revealed new constraints that shaped the direction forward.

Concept 01 — Horizontal Filter Bar with Domain Tabs

Filters placed in a horizontal bar above results, with domain-based tabs to section results by type. Compact layout but quickly degraded under complexity — not enough horizontal space for meaningful options without crowding the interface.

The domain tab logic showed clear promise and evolved into the domain shortcut buttons in the final design.

  • Horizontal filter bar was too limited for complex parameter sets
  • Not mobile friendly
Domain-based navigation as a structural anchor

Concept 02 — Accordion Result Cards

Standard search results with collapsible accordion-style cards, letting users expand individual results for more detail. Dismissed over concern that users might miss critical information if it were collapsed by default.

  • Open/close mechanic was too much risk of hiding critical information
Structured card layout as a foundation

Concept 03 — Table View

Referenced usaspending.gov's data table model. Dense and scannable, but SAM.gov delivers information — context-rich records requiring reading and evaluation — not raw data meant to be sorted and compared.

  • Table format was not suitable for informational content
  • Not mobile friendly
Entire search results are visible and easy to read

06 — Design Solutions

Two decisions. One guiding principle: reduce friction.

01

Restructured result cards for scannability

Each result card was redesigned around a clear visual hierarchy: primary identifier, domain tag, key metadata, and action. Data-dense results are grouped within a consistent card structure so that users can scan vertically without re-reading the layout on each item.

02

Simplified filter panel

All buttons, borders, backgrounds, and actions not directly relevant to filtering were removed. Every element in the filter panel exists to help users find what they need — nothing more. Key filter groups are visible by default, reducing the number of interactions required before a user can begin narrowing results.

07 — Outcomes & Reflection

What shipped looked different from what I proposed.

The client-approved direction prioritized information density — more search parameters surfaced in the filter panel, bolder typographic treatment on result metadata, and additional keyword-matching controls (Any Words / All Words / Exact Phrase). The domain shortcut buttons I had explored in wireframing made it in, but the overall filter experience grew more complex rather than less.

My Proposal Client Approved

My proposed direction made a different bet: that reducing visual noise and surfacing structure clearly would lower cognitive load for a broad public audience — users who arrive with a specific goal but without specialized training. Neither direction is wrong. But they represent genuinely different philosophies about what "useful" means for a search page serving diverse users across 10 federal domains.

Note

The final delivered version diverged from this proposal. This case study presents my recommended direction and the rationale behind it.

What I learned & what I'd do differently

Do next time

Lead with research, not designsOpen every review with competitive analysis and usability findings — ground the conversation in behavior before showing a single screen.
Name the tradeoff explicitly"More information density" vs. "lower cognitive load" is a real design tension. Name it out loud and let the team decide consciously.

What I'd avoid

Deferring when I should pushThis was my first UX project. I can see exactly where I deferred when I should have advocated. The work was there — the advocacy wasn't.
Letting decisions resolve by defaultDirection should be named and agreed upon, not left to resolve passively in the direction of development convenience.
